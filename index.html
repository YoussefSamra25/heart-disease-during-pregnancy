01 / INTRODUCTION

Why pregnancy matters to the heart

Heart disease during pregnancy includes a condition present before pregnancy or one that develops during it, affecting the heart’s structure or function and potentially the health of both mother and fetus.

More circulating blood

Blood volume rises substantially during pregnancy, increasing the demand placed on the cardiovascular system.

A harder-working heart

Heart rate and cardiac output increase to deliver oxygen and nutrients to the growing fetus.

Less room to adapt

A compromised heart may struggle with these changes, increasing the chance of heart failure, arrhythmias, or other complications.

02 / CLASSIFICATION

Different conditions. Different levels of risk.

Clinicians consider the cause and type of heart disease, the person’s functional capacity, and the overall cardiovascular risk of pregnancy.

Congenital heart disease

Structural differences present from birth, including atrial or ventricular septal defects, tetralogy of Fallot, and patent ductus arteriosus. Risk depends on the defect, its severity, pulmonary pressure, and any prior repair.

Acquired heart disease

Conditions developing after birth, including rheumatic valve disease, cardiomyopathy, ischemic heart disease, and hypertensive heart disease.

Examples and clinical context

Congenital defects may involve the chambers, valves, or major vessels. An atrial septal defect (ASD) connects the upper chambers; a ventricular septal defect (VSD) connects the lower chambers. Tetralogy of Fallot can reduce oxygenated blood reaching the body, while patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) is a connection between the aorta and pulmonary artery that remains open after birth. Acquired disease includes rheumatic mitral stenosis, which can raise lung pressure as blood volume rises; cardiomyopathy, which impairs pumping; ischemic disease, which reduces coronary blood supply; and hypertensive disease, which can impair cardiac function.

NYHA describes symptoms and limits on physical activity. mWHO estimates the overall cardiovascular risk associated with pregnancy. Both inform monitoring and care.

03 / RISK FACTORS

What may increase risk?

Risk can reflect a pre-existing heart condition, other health factors, lifestyle, or the demands of a particular pregnancy.

Factors to consider with a care team

Previous heart failure, significant arrhythmia, reduced heart pumping function, pulmonary arterial hypertension, severe valve disease, cyanosis, or aortic enlargement may signal a greater chance of complications.

  • Maternal: age 35 or older, prior cardiovascular disease or surgery, and family history.
  • Medical: hypertension, diabetes, obesity, kidney disease, anemia, and certain autoimmune or thyroid conditions.
  • Pregnancy-related: multiple pregnancy, pre-eclampsia, and previous peripartum cardiomyopathy.
  • Lifestyle: smoking, inactivity, unhealthy diet, and substance use.

Additional factors: dyslipidemia, high parity, assisted reproduction associated with multiple pregnancy, previous thromboembolism or thrombophilia, severe anemia, previous cardiotoxic chemotherapy, and pulmonary hypertension.

Indicators of poorer maternal outcomes: NYHA class III–IV, previous heart failure, left ventricular ejection fraction below 40%, pulmonary arterial hypertension, severe valvular disease, cyanosis, significant arrhythmias, and aortic dilatation.

04 / CLINICAL MANIFESTATIONS

Signs worth paying attention to

Some changes can overlap with normal pregnancy. New, persistent, severe, or worsening symptoms need clinical assessment rather than self-diagnosis.

Breathing changes

Breathlessness, difficulty breathing when lying flat, waking suddenly short of breath, persistent cough, or lung crackles.

Heart and circulation

Palpitations, persistent fast heartbeat, chest discomfort, fainting, bluish discoloration, or a new concerning murmur.

Daily function

Excessive fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, or marked or rapidly increasing swelling.

More clinical findings

Orthopnea means breathlessness while lying flat; paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea wakes a person from sleep with sudden breathlessness. Pulmonary congestion can cause night cough and crackles. Raised jugular venous pressure may suggest right-sided heart failure. Syncope can reflect reduced cerebral blood flow, while hemoptysis (blood-stained sputum) is uncommon in uncomplicated disease. A murmur may also occur with normal pregnancy blood-flow changes, so assessment matters. Acute pulmonary edema can present with severe breathlessness, rapid breathing, crackles, low oxygen, anxiety, and pink frothy sputum.

Warning signs: seek immediate medical assessment

These symptoms may indicate an urgent cardiac or respiratory problem:

  • Severe shortness of breath, especially at rest
  • Chest pain or pressure
  • Persistent or severe palpitations
  • Fainting or severe dizziness
  • Suddenly worsening swelling
  • Bluish lips or skin
  • Cough with pink, frothy sputum
05 / INVESTIGATIONS

Building a clearer picture

Assessment aims to identify the underlying condition, evaluate cardiac function and severity, and check maternal and fetal well-being.

Clinical assessment

Medical and pregnancy history, current medicines, vital signs, oxygen saturation, examination for heart failure, and listening for murmurs or irregular rhythms.

Laboratory tests

Complete blood count, kidney function, and electrolytes; BNP or NT-proBNP when heart failure is suspected; troponin when myocardial injury is suspected.

Cardiac tests and imaging

ECG checks rhythm, conduction and signs of strain. Echocardiography is first-line cardiac imaging in pregnancy, assessing chambers, valves and pumping function. Holter monitoring records rhythm for 24–48 hours or longer to capture intermittent arrhythmias. Chest X-ray may assess cardiomegaly or lung congestion when indicated. Cardiac MRI can clarify findings when echocardiography is insufficient; non-contrast MRI is preferred during pregnancy.

Fetal assessment

Obstetric ultrasound evaluates growth and development. Fetal echocardiography may assess heart structure when risk is increased; fetal heart-rate monitoring is used when indicated.

06 / MEDICAL & NURSING MANAGEMENT

Coordinated care, from planning to postpartum

Treatment is individualized to the heart condition, pregnancy stage, and maternal and fetal needs. A multidisciplinary team can plan monitoring, medication, delivery, and follow-up.

Before & during pregnancy

Pre-pregnancy counselling helps clarify risk. Regular follow-up can monitor symptoms, blood pressure, oxygenation, cardiac function, and fetal growth.

Medication review

Clinicians may use selected diuretics, beta-blockers, anticoagulants, digoxin, or vasodilators when appropriate. ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and direct renin inhibitors are contraindicated during pregnancy.

Delivery & after birth

Delivery plans depend on cardiac and obstetric needs. Close postpartum monitoring matters because circulation changes rapidly after delivery.

Medication and delivery considerations

When prescribed, diuretics such as furosemide can address fluid overload, but excess diuresis may reduce placental blood flow. Beta-blockers such as metoprolol may control heart rate with fetal growth monitoring. Anticoagulation for mechanical valves or thromboembolic conditions requires individualized choice by pregnancy stage; digoxin and vasodilators may be used in selected cases. For many patients vaginal delivery is preferred; cesarean delivery is reserved for specific cardiac or obstetric indications. Analgesia, assisted delivery, and continuous monitoring may be considered according to risk.

The nursing focus

Observe and respond

Monitor vital signs, breathing, fluid balance, edema, pain, palpitations, and fetal well-being. Report sudden deterioration promptly.

Support and educate

Promote appropriate rest and positioning, administer prescribed treatment safely, offer emotional support, and explain symptoms that require urgent assessment.

Assessment, rest and oxygenation

Monitor heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, intake and output, edema, crackles, chest pain and fetal well-being. Support rest, avoid excessive exertion, offer positioning such as semi-Fowler’s for breathlessness, and give prescribed oxygen when indicated. Report sudden respiratory deterioration promptly.

Fluids, medication and education

Track fluid balance, sudden weight gain and anemia; follow prescribed sodium or fluid limits. Check response and adverse effects of prescribed medicines, including bleeding with anticoagulants. Explain the treatment plan, follow-up and urgent warning signs, and offer family and psychological support.

Labor and postpartum nursing

During labor monitor maternal vital signs and fetal heart rate, manage fluids carefully and support prescribed pain relief and comfortable positioning. After birth assess breathing, edema, bleeding and fluid balance; encourage mobilization when medically appropriate and arrange cardiac follow-up. Circulatory shifts make the first 24–72 hours after delivery particularly important.

Expected outcomes

Goals include stable maternal cardiac function, adequate maternal and fetal oxygenation, fetal growth where possible, safe delivery with minimal cardiac stress, prevention of postpartum complications, and improved quality of life.

07 / COMPLICATIONS

Why ongoing monitoring matters

Complications can affect the mother, pregnancy, or baby. Timely assessment and a tailored plan help the care team recognize and respond to problems.

Maternal

Heart failure, pulmonary edema, arrhythmias, blood clots, ischemia, stroke, infection of the heart lining, and low oxygen levels.

Obstetric

Preterm labor, pre-eclampsia, reduced placental blood flow, and cardiac deterioration after delivery.

Fetal & neonatal

Restricted growth, low birth weight, reduced oxygen supply, prematurity, neonatal breathing difficulties, and increased congenital heart disease risk in some families.

Additional possible outcomes

Maternal complications may also include myocardial infarction, systemic embolism, infective endocarditis, and, in severe cases, cardiac arrest or death. Placental insufficiency may affect fetal growth and oxygen delivery. Fetal or neonatal complications can include fetal distress, intrauterine fetal death, and respiratory distress associated with prematurity. Offspring of mothers with congenital heart disease may need fetal cardiac assessment.
